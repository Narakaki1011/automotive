How much should you actually be saving?

The goal you described: reach a point where the fund buys every future vehicle on its own, with no more monthly contributions needed. Here's what that takes given the real prices and the real timeline.

  • 1
    The truck's 2033 purchase is already on track
    At today's $300/mo and 10% average returns, the fund covers the Raptor's $65,000 in 2033 with about $8,750 left over. The near-term goal doesn't need a change.
  • 2
    Funding both vehicles forever, on a 10-year cycle, takes about $390/mo
    Not $300. The gap shows up later: the SUV purchase in the early-to-mid 2040s, plus repeating both every 10 years after that, is where today's contribution falls short. About $90/mo more closes it, assuming you keep contributing that amount indefinitely.
  • 3
    Stretching to a 12-year cycle brings that down to about $340/mo
    A smaller step up from today, and realistic given how lightly both vehicles are actually driven.
  • 4
    A lump sum is still the fastest single lever
    Money in earlier compounds longer. A $10,000 bonus or tax refund dropped in now closes more of the gap than an equivalent raise in monthly contributions spread over the same years.
  • 5
    "Never save again" is a balance target, not just a contribution rate
    At 10% growth, roughly $70,000 to $75,000 pooled in the fund is what lets both vehicles' replacement cycles regenerate on growth alone. You're at about $19,900 today; use the simulator above to see how contribution size and any lump sum change when that's reached.
Balance model: start-of-2025 balance $15,200, compounding annually at a flat 10% return, plus 12 times the monthly contribution each year, any one-time lump sum in its chosen year, and a withdrawal of the stated cost in each vehicle's replacement year and every cycle-length interval after. Repair history

Watch the Jeep's 74,000 to 79,000 mile window. A transmission/transfer-case/differential service ($1,219), header bolts ($1,400), and an engine mount ($585) all landed inside a single 5,000-mile stretch in 2024/25: $3,204 in three repairs, against $100 oil changes everywhere else on the timeline. Worth asking your mechanic whether one root cause tied those together, so you're not paying to fix the same problem three times.
The truck's $1,920 "maintenance" was a one-time recon, not a run rate. Transmission service, battery, windshield, and front brakes were all done the month it was bought. Its actual forward maintenance is just the oil changes below, about $200/yr.
Maintained on a tighter schedule than most owners run. Both vehicles get transmission fluid flushes every 50,000 miles (not the 100k+ many owners stretch to) and spark plugs around 100,000 miles. It costs more up front, but it's a real part of why these two are worth keeping this long at such low annual mileage; see the schedule below.

EV comparison

Model Y vs. the alternatives: a cost comparison.

Two ways to think about replacing the Jeep: a same-size hybrid SUV, or a same-size gas V8 like the one currently in the driveway but seven years newer. Both are compared against a Model Y, Long Range or Performance, Sammamish assumptions used everywhere else in this dashboard by default, over whatever location, EV charging window, annual mileage, and holding period you set below (off-peak charging, 15,000 mi/yr, 8-year hold by default). Only the two Tesla trims are affected by the charging-window choice; the Highlander and Grand Cherokee run on gas.

Both Model Y trims are always shown alongside whichever alternative is selected, so a near-tie stays visible without extra clicks. Resale value is shown as a credit, a negative segment, against the gross cost.

Tesla resale has a real cliff, not just a discount. CarEdge's year-by-year data shows Model Y residual value dropping from 38.9% in year 5 to 29.3% in year 6, a 9.6-point single-year drop that lines up with the 8-year/120,000-mile battery and drivetrain warranty aging out. The Highlander's depreciation curve has no equivalent cliff, just a smooth 5 to 6 points a year the whole way. That gap is why Tesla resale runs weaker here, not a generic assumption.
Least certain number on this chart: the 2021 Summit V8 is already about 5 years old today; holding it 8 more years pushes it to age 13, one year past CarEdge's own 12-year reliable-data ceiling. The $13,800 resale figure required extrapolating the flattening trend from years 9 through 12 forward, and there's anchor ambiguity too, since today's actual value tracks closer to CarEdge's age-4 row than its age-5 row. Real risk skews further downward given the WK2 Summit's known post-warranty air-suspension and electronics repair reputation, which isn't priced into maintenance at all.
